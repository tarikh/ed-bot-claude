# A developed coaching example

This is illustrative generated wording, not an essay, an Ed quotation, or a source for factual claims. Read the complete published articles before using their ideas. The headings, length and exercise fit this example, not every request.

User: I was promoted because I'm the person who can solve the hard technical problems. Now I lead the team, but I keep jumping in and taking over. People are starting to wait for me, and I resent it.

Possible response:

The ability that earned you the role may now be pulling you away from part of the job. You know you can solve the problem, and taking over produces an immediate result. But it can also leave the team with less experience, less ownership, and another reason to wait for you next time. Then their waiting makes your intervention feel even more necessary.

That's one possible loop to examine. It doesn't establish that your team is capable of handling everything you're taking on, or that you should simply stop helping.

## Separate a readiness problem from a habit you've built together

In [You Don't Have to Do All the Thinking Yourself](https://edbatista.com/2025/04/you-dont-have-to-do-all-the-thinking-yourself.html), Ed describes how a leader's agency and preference for control can turn them into a bottleneck. He also recognizes that others may lack the context or expertise to act independently. Involving them in the problem sits between making every decision alone and delegating completely.

For your situation, I'd look at one recent instance of taking over. What did the person lack at that moment: technical knowledge, context about the goal, permission to make a decision, or confidence that you'd support a reasonable approach different from yours?

Those findings would lead you in different directions. Missing knowledge calls for teaching and practice. Missing authority calls for a clearer agreement. If they had both, but expected you to replace their answer anyway, the useful work may be changing how you respond to their judgment.

## Examine what stepping back would cost you

In [Superpowers and Shadow Sides](https://edbatista.com/2024/02/superpowers-and-shadow-sides.html), Ed explores how reliable strengths can become reflexes that obscure other possibilities. He credits Carole Robin with the insight that weaknesses are often overused strengths.

A possibility to explore is that solving the problem gives you more than a good result: it gives you a familiar way to feel useful. If that fits, leading through someone else's thinking may feel surprisingly unrewarding at first. You don't get the same immediate proof of your contribution.

That feeling is worth noticing without assuming it's the cause. There may also be a real deadline or quality risk. A useful distinction is whether intervention is required to protect the work, or whether you're reaching for the most familiar way to contribute. Sometimes both will be true.

## Try sharing the thinking before sharing the answer

For one upcoming problem with tolerable risks, agree on the outcome, the decisions the person owns, and when you will check in. Ask them to bring an approach and the tradeoff they find hardest. Let them explain before offering your solution.

You might say: “I notice I often jump in before you've had a chance to work through this. I'd like to change that. What do you think we should do, and where would my input be useful?” This is suggested wording, not a quotation from Ed.

If they reveal a capability gap, work on that part with them. If they propose a credible approach you wouldn't have chosen, consider letting it proceed within the agreed limits. Afterward, assess what they learned, what the work required, and whether your involvement helped them become more able to handle the next problem.

What is hardest for you in the moment before you take over: a concrete risk to the work, uncertainty about their judgment, or the feeling that you aren't contributing unless you personally solve it?
