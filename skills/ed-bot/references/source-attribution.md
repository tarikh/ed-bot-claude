- Scope limit: the **Rival** section describes feeling overshadowed or unappreciated, a perceived rightful claim to the CEO position, resentment and power struggles. It does not identify ambition as an original constructive strength. When describing all five types, give these supported observations in prose. Do not invent an “original strength” column for the Rival to complete a uniform table. If the user asks for that missing field, say the essay does not establish it. A separate hypothesis about this user's ambition is an assistant application, not Ed's account.
- CEO-side causes remain distinct: a high need for control can produce directive/forceful behavior; scarce candid upward feedback contributes to a blind spot. One is not established as the cause of the other.

## Distinctive contributions retain their origin

- **Mind the Gap (On Leading Senior Executives) (4144):** The exact three section headings are **Accountability, Motivation, Team Dynamics**, in that order. Ed discusses how CEOs leave gaps when leading experienced executives: autonomy still needs agreed objectives and accountability; motivation varies and includes satisfaction as well as dissatisfaction; senior hires affect norms, culture and collective identity. “Development” is not the third source category. A development practice can be the assistant's separately identified application. Preserve **Peter Drucker** for knowledge workers, **Douglas McGregor** for Theory X/Y, and **Frederick Herzberg** for the two-factor account of job satisfaction/dissatisfaction when using those specific contributions. Canonical URL: https://edbatista.com/2022/04/mind-the-gap-on-leading-senior-executives.html.
- **How to Deliver Critical Feedback (4059):** Two original contributions have different credits. **David Bradford and Allan Cohen** developed *supportive confrontation*: impact on me, inconsistency with the person's apparent goals, costs even when goals are achieved, and examining my part in the problem. Within that essay's first section, **David Bradford and Mary Ann Huckabay** supply the distinct “net” model: observed behavior and experienced emotion/impact are known; motives and intentions are inferred. Credit Bradford and Huckabay when presenting that distinction, even if supportive confrontation has already been credited to Bradford and Cohen.
- **How to Have Better One-on-Ones (4200):** **Andy Grove and Claire Hughes Johnson** are the named origins Ed draws on for employee-owned agendas. Name both when presenting that principle. Grove relates cadence to experience with the specific task and recommends an hour minimum; Ed describes varying cadences and no universal template. A shorter suggested agenda is the assistant's application, not Grove's prescription. Agenda preparation can reveal whether a report has prepared; an assessment of their broader judgment is a separate managerial application, not a finding Ed makes in that essay.

- **Mourning Ends, Grief Need Not (4015):** Ed explicitly honors **Keia Cole** as the source of the distinction between mourning that ends as we return to the world and grief we may revisit indefinitely. Name Cole when presenting that distinction, including in a broad overview about death. **Bonnie Wentworth** said “Grief is non-linear.” Ed's father-in-law was Richard Wright; the student was Roanak Desai. Ed's personal narrative and gratitude are his; Sage Cohen and Elliott Jaques have separately credited passages. A compact useful form is “Ed shares Keia Cole's distinction between mourning and continuing grief.”
- **Leadership and the Double Bind (3929):** **Gregory Bateson and colleagues** developed the double-bind concept. Preserve their credit when explaining incompatible messages/demands that create a trap. Ed applies it to leadership and proposes acceptance, avoiding blame and emotion regulation. His application does not transfer the concept's origin to him. The responsible/victim-mindset definitions are the **Trium Group**'s; quoted experiment is recounted by **Paul Watzlawick**.
- **Give It To Me Straight (Effective Feedback) (3788):** **David Bradford and Mary Ann Huckabay** supply “the net”: observed behavior and our experienced impact are known; the other person's motives are guesses. Name them even if paraphrasing this pattern without the net metaphor. Example credit: “Ed draws on David Bradford and Mary Ann Huckabay's distinction between observed behavior, experienced impact and inferred motives.” **David Rock** developed SCARF. **John Gottman**'s friendship factors and positive/negative ratio originate in research on married/committed couples; Ed proposes a workplace application. The ratio is not five compliments required before one criticism. Ed's discussion of candor and calibrated emotion is his synthesis; not every general communication point needs a framework label.
- **You Don't Have to Do All the Thinking Yourself (4232):** **Carole Robin** is explicitly credited for weaknesses as overused strengths. Name her for that principle. **Jerry Burger**'s cited study describes people with high desire for control, not an empirical population of all founders/CEOs. **David McClelland** developed motivational-needs theory and leadership-motive syndrome; preserve those origins and populations. **Michael Watkins** is the source for the quoted culture definition. Ed's practical invitation to share problem-solving is his application.
- **The Antidote to "Manager Mode" is Actual Management (4240):** **Douglas McGregor** developed Theory X/Y; **Amy Edmondson** is the identified psychological-safety researcher. Ed's concrete discussion of ownership, deadlines, standards, check-ins and intervention is his synthesis.

These cards do not require adding every listed name. Include only the originator of the specific idea actually used. Retain substantive coaching; a brief credit can sit within the explanation. Never omit required credit merely to fit a word cap; shorten other prose instead.

- **Superpowers and Shadow Sides (4228), and its cross-references:** **Carole Robin** is explicitly named for the context-dependent overused-strengths dynamic. Preserve this origin when an essay such as *Working with the "Difficult" Co-Founder* (18113) points back to the principle. In 18113, high need for control can make a CEO seem directive or autocratic; insufficient upward feedback contributes to unrecognized impact. Do not compress those observations into a single asserted causal finding. A possibility about this user's self-awareness is a separately marked coaching application.
- **Merciful Exits (On Under-Performing Executives) (4074):** Ed poses “What do you owe this person?” and “What do you NOT owe them?” The honesty/fairness/dignity and permanent-role/special-treatment/debate lists are his **CEO clients' consistent responses**, shared by Ed. Keep that provenance when presenting the distinctive parallel lists. Peter Drucker supplies the quoted argument about the cruelty of keeping someone in a job they cannot do; Lee Ross is credited for fundamental attribution error.

- **Accountability and Empathy (Are Not Mutually Exclusive) (3993):** Ed explicitly calls his graphics simplified versions of **Jane Mouton and Robert Blake's Mouton–Blake Managerial Grid**. Credit both the underlying model and Ed's accountability–empathy adaptation when presenting its axes or quadrants, including in a diagram caption. A compact caption is “Adapted from Ed Batista's application of Jane Mouton and Robert Blake's managerial grid,” beside the full-title source link. Ed reverses the original grid's axes and simplifies its categories; the original has a 1–9 scale and a middle-of-the-road category. If you simplify it further or rename quadrants, label those choices as your adaptation. High accountability and high empathy describe the desired culture; they do not guarantee performance.
