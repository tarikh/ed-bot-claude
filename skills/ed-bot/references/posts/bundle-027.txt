Yesterday's SF Chronicle had a slightly-behind-the-times but still enjoyable article on small-business blogging by Ilana DeBare. Yes, even small businesses are blogging--who knew? But one passage did jump out at me:

Folger Levin & Kahn, a midsize San Francisco law firm, started an

intellectual property law blog in March (www.iplawobserver.com) as a way to

showcase its IP knowledge to potential clients.

Folger attorneys spend a total of 10 to 15 hours per week writing blog

entries about recent appellate court decisions on IP law.

"A lot of law firm business is based on word-of-mouth, where someone

tells a friend about Folger," said Gregory Call, a Folger partner. "That works

very well with my generation, which is 45 and over. But what's the equivalent

of word-of-mouth for younger people today, many of whom are moving into

decision-making positions? Maybe a blog will do that .... I don't think people

will hire us just because we have a blog, but they may talk to us because we

have a blog."

Now this is actually news. As the husband of a former corporate attorney, I'm well aware that a law firm isn't going to devote 10 to 15 hours a week of billable associate time to blogging because it's the cool thing to do. They're doing it because it pays. Blogging actually has a positive ROI. Well, depending on your blog.

title: The Big Bang vs. USP
url: https://edbatista.com/2005/05/big_bang_vs_usp.html
slug: big_bang_vs_usp
published: 2005-05-05T14:32:52
updated: 2005-05-05T14:32:52
source_body: wordpress-html
---

The Big Bang theory of advertising, expounded by people like Linda Kaplan Thaler, asserts that only those ideas that are "too outrageous, too different, too polarizing to go unnoticed" will break through the "sea of sameness out there."  (Quoted in Knowledge@Wharton.)  The Big Bang poster child is Thaler's AFLAC duck, an essentially meaningless ad campaign that nevertheless generated so much buzz that the company remade its logo in the duck's image and now sells an assortment of duck gear (with the proceeds going to charity.)

The countervailing theory is the Unique Selling Proposition, a term coined by Rosser Reeves in the '50s and popularized by Jay Abraham.  The USP can be summarized as "Buy this product, and you will get this specific benefit," a proposition "that the competition either cannot, or does not, offer."

Obviously, there are other approaches marketers can choose, but let's say the Big Bang and the USP represent opposite ends of a spectrum.  Which one makes more sense for nonprofits?  It's easy to say neither.  Few nonprofits have the money it takes to support a sustained Big Bang campaign, and just as few are able to articulate a USP for their organization in opposition to other issues or to their "competitors.  But let's reduce these concepts to their essentials to make them a little more nonprofit-friendly.

The essence of the Big Bang is: Stand out.  Do whatever it takes to get noticed.  Let your freak flag fly.  Nonprofits can do this, and they can do it well; PETA comes to mind, with Pamela Anderson's Explicit New Video and The Naked Truth about Ringling Bros.  (I may disagree with their aims, but I applaud their methods.)

The essence of the UPS is: You need us, and we're better than they are.  Not every nonprofit has the guts to pull this off, but it's the implicit message in every alumni fundraising campaign, and in plenty of health and disease-related campaigns as well.

Is the best approach always contextual?  Perhaps.  But wouldn't it be great to to see a university do something freaky, just to get attention.  Wouldn't you pay attention if a social services org said, "We'll kick the competition's ass!"  Just wondering.

(Hat tip to the February 28 issue of The New Yorker, which featured a Ken Auletta article on Thaler,  the Big Bang approach, and its limitations in a fragmented media environment.)

title: Shame
url: https://edbatista.com/2005/05/shame.html

published: 2005-05-04T21:18:34
updated: 2005-05-04T21:18:34
source_body: wordpress-html

Today's SF Chronicle features the first installment in a three-part series by Mike Weiss on the scandal surrounding the James Beard Foundation,  which is "dedicated to furthering the practice and appreciation of the culinary arts in America."  Len Pickell, who had served the Foundation as volunteer, fundraiser, and eventually president, apparently spent more than $1 million on personal expenses.

But this isn't just your run-of-the-mill nonprofit mismanagement story.  The bizarre twist is that Pickell refused to accept a salary from the Foundation, claiming (falsely) that he was independently wealthy and didn't need the money!  Pickell was apparently a skilled administrator and charismatic fundraiser, and played a major role in the Foundation's growth over the past two decades.  He probably could have commanded a salary well over six figures and actually earned all the money he stole.  Instead, he couldn't escape his own lies, or perhaps he just found them thrilling.  Either way, it's a sad story with unhappy endings all around, not least for all the well-run nonprofits whose own fundraising will take a hit in the wake of one more scandal.

Pickell comes off as a likeable rogue, but the truth is that he abused the trust that all donors place in nonprofits, and other nonprofits will suffer as a result.  The scale of his misdeeds is piddling compared with the corporate scandals littering the news these days, but people don't stop investing when they hear about accounting tricks--they just move their money elsewhere (assuming they still have it.)  People do stop giving when they hear about nonprofit scandals.  Shame on him, and shame on a Board of Directors that was clearly asleep at the wheel.

UPDATE: Part Two in the Chronicle series.

UPDATE REDUX: Part Three in the series.  Money quote No. 1:

[Edna] Morris [,the Foundation's new Executive Director,] is getting $20,000 a month, plus the rent on a one-bedroom Greenwich Village apartment, where rents are typically $3,000 to $4,000. She is only in the job temporarily--to clean house, [former Board Chairman George] Sape says. With Beard picking up the tab, she continues to commute frequently to her home in Orlando.

All told, says one foundation insider who wants to remain anonymous but is voicing an oft-expressed view, "what she's getting is about the same amount Len was skimming."

And No. 2:

There remains the mystery of why Len Pickell  --  the son of dry cleaners, the househusband who doted on his daughters, the amateur oenophile  --  refused a salary, a six-figure salary, when the foundation he was building up  by leaps and bounds was willing to offer him one. Even his lawyer doesn't get that. "I said to his doctor," [attorney Stacey] Richman says, " 'Can you get me an answer to this question?' Because this is killing me."

A damn shame.

title: Integrated?  Who, Me?
url: https://edbatista.com/2005/05/integrated_who_.html
slug: integrated_who_
published: 2005-05-04T19:47:07
updated: 2005-05-04T19:47:07
source_body: wordpress-html
---

We all talk about online/offline integration when it comes to fundraising and advocacy campaigns, but who's really doing it?  Somebody must be, but I haven't met them.  We've advanced to the point where well-run nonprofits recognize that they should have an integrated communication strategy that addresses both online and offline channels.  But we're still a long way from the Holy Grail of an integrated CRM platform that an organization can use to manage their online and offline contacts, messages, events, etc.

Online fundraising is only about 4% of the overall total, but it's been doubling for four years straight, and it doesn't seem to be slackening.  By the time it levels off (and it will--paper may be losing market share, but it's not going to disappear), nonprofits will be desperate for a truly integrated set of tools that minimize overlap and inefficiency and maximize returns across online and offline communications.

I know next to nothing about direct mail, but it strikes me as a fairly straightforward business, with well-developed metrics and methodologies.  And as I learned today from online fundraising guru Nick Allen, the nonproft direct mail market is a relatively fragmented one, with a number of small providers.

Given that the market of online CRM providers is more consolidated, and that the tools are still maturing, it would seem to make sense for one of these companies to acquire a direct mail provider and really, truly integrate their services.  Convio, GetActive and Kintera all have their own version of partner networks that include "direct marketing" providers, but these appear to be relatively loose arrangements.  I know these folks must be working on something more substantial in this area, but I haven't seen it yet, and I'd really like to.

(On a totally unrelated note, what's up with that woman on the Kintera partner page?  She looks like she's going to make you an offer you can't refuse.  I'm not really feeling warm partnership fuzzies here.  Folks, just Google image "partner" and you'll find dozens of friendlier images.  You'll also find heavy-set, tattooed guys on the beach with their girlfriends and monkeys riding a tandem bicycle, but you get the idea.)
