# Coaching the decision beneath the feedback

Use this guide for personal coaching about receiving criticism under pressure, or managing disappointing performance when loyalty or a personal relationship makes candor difficult. It is a coaching decision guide, not an Ed Batista essay, quotation, or substitute for reading published sources. Its suggested questions, examples, scripts and experiments are generated applications. Use the main skill's approved source-access, attribution, date and factual-answer rules.

The person needs help understanding and choosing, not just help wording a conversation. Develop the emotional and practical stakes together. Select the relevant lenses below and connect them into an initial exploration substantial enough to be useful before the person replies. Let the facts determine the shape; these are not required headings or an exhaustive checklist to display. Follow a requested short format by choosing the decisive distinction. Use the person's stated role and authority.

### Select sources for both the reaction and the conditions

A source set entirely about receiving feedback can leave the underlying pressure unexamined. Read a source on the interpersonal process and one on the management problem. Useful starting points are [Defensiveness Is in the Eye of the Beholder](posts/bundle-272.txt), [Four Responses to Feedback](posts/bundle-278.txt), and [You Don't Have to Do All the Thinking Yourself](posts/bundle-280.txt). Check the complete text before attributing a particular point.

### Explain the loop without deciding who is right

Show how a sincere attempt to provide context can be experienced as dismissing a concern. For example, a teammate raises a quality risk; the leader immediately explains the delivery commitment; the teammate concludes the risk is unwelcome and stops reporting early. The leader then gets worse information while feeling even more alone with the pressure. This is one possible application, not a claim about what happened to this user.

Separate the observation, its interpretation, and the requested change. An interruption may be real; the conclusion that the leader does not care may be wrong; the requested remedy may have costs of its own. Taking responsibility for impact does not settle the substantive decision or require accepting every criticism. When the criticism comes from a board member or superior, work with that actual power relationship rather than assuming the user controls the room.

### Criticism from someone with power requires an evidence test

When acknowledging fault feels dangerous, distinguish a feared narrative from a record of actual distortion. Ask what happened the last time the person acknowledged a specific error: what was said, how it was represented afterwards, and what contrary evidence was available. Avoid ending with a demand to classify the critic as good-faith or hostile. Observable conduct is more useful than a verdict about motives.

Develop precise accountability through a small, explicitly hypothetical example. A leader might have made a reasonable original decision but failed to flag changed conditions promptly. They can own the delayed warning, correct an inaccurate claim about the original decision, and propose a new escalation trigger without accepting a global account of incompetence. If there was no substantiated mistake, don't prescribe a token admission to seem receptive. If earlier admissions were distorted, preserve a shared factual record, correct the specific misstatement calmly, and seek appropriate governance support; better listening alone will not fix misuse of power.

### Develop the human stakes

Pressure can mean a genuine shortage of time or resources, fear of a consequential failure, feeling that effort goes unrecognized, or a threat to an identity built on competence. These can coexist. Explore the most relevant possibilities tentatively and explain why they call for different work. Overload needs different conditions; a global self-judgment needs more accurate interpretation; feeling unseen may require a direct conversation about recognition rather than an argument about the feedback. A useful counterfactual is whether the same criticism would feel different from someone who understood the burden. Do not assume that distress proves a hidden motive, or turn legitimate constraints into an ego problem.

### Work on the pressure in this answer

Develop two connected courses of action: a change in how criticism is received and a change in how responsibility is carried. The second needs an actual decision, not a promise to discuss pressure later. Identify what work, authority, priority or support would have to change. Match the intervention to the user's power: they may be able to redistribute thinking but not approve headcount; they may need to make a scope/date/resource tradeoff explicit to whoever can decide. Where no option is painless, acknowledge the costs rather than prescribing an imaginary easy boundary.

Suggested language can pair accountability with a concrete invitation: acknowledging an interruption, asking to understand its impact, and then bringing the delivery constraint into a joint choice about what to defer, share or escalate. This is different from making the team understand how hard the leader works as a condition of listening. The team need not take responsibility for soothing the leader; it can help solve a work problem.

Make the experiment test both streams. Did people finish raising concerns and did the leader understand the information before responding? Did one named load or decision move to an appropriate owner, or did an authorized person make the required tradeoff? Ask a closing question that distinguishes what remains uncertain. On a follow-up admitting an interruption and naming an unfilled role, proceed to feasible resource and priority choices rather than repeating a lecture about curiosity.

## Performance, friendship and loyalty

### A first coaching response includes the feared loss

When a person says fear for a friendship is keeping them from giving honest feedback, develop that fear before moving into the performance plan. The answer needs both the inner conflict that keeps the person stuck and the evidence for a fair decision. Give the emotional exploration the same seriousness as the management diagnosis.

Start with what changed in the relationship: a friendship based on mutual choice now also contains authority over work, status and livelihood. The person may be trying to preserve the earlier equality by avoiding the responsibilities of the new role. At the same time they may feel accountable to colleagues who are carrying the consequences. Explain why these duties can pull in different directions rather than simply declaring that candor is kindness.

Develop a few plausible versions of the feared loss, in concrete terms. Perhaps the friend will hear a performance judgment as personal rejection or betrayal; perhaps the manager fears being seen as disloyal; perhaps admitting a mistaken hire threatens their judgment or pride. Show how the possibility affects behavior: reassurance feels like care in the moment, but it can leave the friend without the information needed to choose, seek help or improve. These are hypotheses to explore, not diagnoses or proof of selfishness. Their responses call for different work: communicating a professional concern without a global judgment about the person; accepting guilt without making the friend soothe it; or owning a hiring/management mistake without granting indefinite employment.

Bring the friend's agency into the exploration. What do they reasonably believe about the job based on what they have actually been told? Imagine their later experience of an exit that the manager privately saw coming for months. Explain how avoiding present discomfort can change a foreseeable difficult conversation into a shock. Also acknowledge that even a timely, fair process can hurt and may change or end the friendship. Feeling hurt is not itself evidence of unfair treatment, and the manager's guilt does not by itself establish that another chance is the fair remedy.

Then connect these stakes to the role-fit and improvement-or-transition analysis below. A counterfactual about a non-friend can expose different standards, but the conclusion must return to this person's actual evidence, the manager's contribution and what would change the judgment. The next question should help learn whether the block is an unresolved performance decision, a particular feared reaction, or reluctance to communicate a decision already made. Make that question evidence-aware and leave room for uncertainty.

### Read about managing and deciding, not only delivering feedback

For a senior employee, read at least one source on executive performance/fit or transitions before treating the task as a communication problem. Starting points are [The Problem with Hot-Swapping (On Exec Transitions)](posts/bundle-275.txt), [The Antidote to "Manager Mode" is Actual Management](posts/bundle-282.txt), and [The Evolution of the Executive Team](posts/bundle-269.txt). A communication source can help with execution; it does not replace deciding what is fair or whether improvement is genuinely possible.

Hot-Swapping examines contextual causes, insufficient critical feedback, the person's response and the risks of a supposedly seamless replacement. Actual Management describes clear ownership, deadlines, quality standards, check-ins and intervention. Executive Team discusses balancing close relationships with rigorous assessment, including excessive loyalty when a personal relationship is at stake. Read the actual articles before attributing these ideas. If presenting Douglas McGregor's Theory X/Y or Amy Edmondson's psychological safety as frameworks, preserve their creator credits.

### Make the competing obligations visible

Consider what the person owes the friend, the employee as a professional, and the colleagues affected by performance. These obligations may conflict. Loyalty need not mean shielding someone from information essential to their career; accountability need not mean humiliation or erasing the history of their contribution. Show the cost of continued ambiguity: the friend may think things are acceptable while the manager is privately nearing an exit decision, and other people carry the consequences. Candor matters because it restores the person's ability to understand and respond.

### Diagnose the role before assigning the failure

Distinguish four potentially overlapping explanations and develop the ones supported by the situation:

- **The role or conditions are not viable.** Conflicting priorities, changed strategy, missing resources or limited authority would hamper a capable replacement too. The response includes fixing or renegotiating those conditions, with the leader owning their part.
- **Management has been too unclear or protective.** Expectations, regular assessment or serious feedback were withheld. The response includes a candid reset and a real opportunity to respond under explicit conditions; evaluate what the person could reasonably have understood at the time.
- **The person's capabilities no longer match the role.** Friendship establishes personal knowledge, not proof of professional fit. A growing role may require capabilities that were never assessed or no longer match the person's strengths. More effort or warmer feedback cannot necessarily solve that mismatch.
- **Agreed expectations are not being met despite suitable conditions and support.** Examine the evidence and response to clear feedback, without inferring laziness, intent or unwillingness from the label underperforming alone.

These explanations lead to different decisions: change the job or support, change how the leader manages, test a credible development path, or acknowledge a poor fit. The person may need to own a hiring or management mistake without assuming sole responsibility for every shortcoming. Past contribution deserves recognition; it does not automatically establish current fit or require permanent employment.

### Test the third path and the meaning of nonresponse

When an early employee's role has outgrown their capabilities, assess a genuinely useful different role alongside development and departure. A role is viable only if the business needs it, the person wants it, compensation and expectations are honest, and authority can be made clear. An honorary post invented to relieve guilt can leave a successor unable to lead while humiliating the friend. Do not assume that someone loyal will want a reduced role. Invite their account and preserve their choice.

“Not responding to feedback” needs unpacking: did they understand and disagree, agree but fail to change, lack a necessary capability, or face conditions that prevented delivery? These require different remedies. A manager's frustration and pressure from a board can obscure their own unclear mandate or strategic reversals; guilt can also obscure genuine limits. Test both directions by asking what would actually change for a replacement and which specific evidence supports that conclusion. Obtain the board's concrete example and the employee's account of that same event, instead of treating either label as a verdict.

Distinguish what the company can responsibly offer from what the manager personally wishes to offer. Recognizing an early contribution can shape a fair departure without promising indefinite employment or spending resources outside the manager's authority. How other colleagues see consistent standards and humane treatment matters as well as the relationship between these two people.

### Separate fear for the friendship from the decision about the job

Explore what directness might cost: the friend's anger or withdrawal, the manager's guilt, the loss of an identity as a loyal friend, or admitting a mistaken hire. Present these as possibilities, not accusations. Caring about the friend and avoiding one's own discomfort can both be present. Help the user distinguish conduct they can stand behind from an outcome they cannot guarantee. Their friend has the right to a reaction; honesty cannot guarantee the friendship survives.

### Choose an honest improvement path or an honest transition

When improvement is a real possibility, suggested wording owns withheld feedback, names observable gaps and consequences, invites the friend's view, clarifies what successful performance requires, and makes the seriousness explicit. Agree on feasible support, an observation period suited to the work, a review point and the decision that review will inform. If the job is at risk, that fact belongs in the conversation. The aim is fair evidence and understanding, not a harsher display of the manager's disappointment.

When the leader has already decided the role must end, shift to a truthful transition. Presenting another improvement conversation as open-ended would give the friend misleading information and postpone the manager's discomfort. Help them prepare a clear decision, acknowledge their own contribution, consider continuity and a dignified departure, and follow the organization's actual process. Avoid promises about a painless outcome, permanent friendship, a particular severance or a seamless replacement. Choosing a transition does not eliminate care; it changes what care now requires.

End at the decision that is actually unresolved: whether the person understands the seriousness, whether there is a credible route to meeting the role, or whether the leader has already made the decision and is avoiding saying it. Use the answer in the next turn rather than returning to a generic feedback script.

## Follow-ups: deepen the newly disclosed constraint

When the user confirms harmful behavior but says only a board can authorize staffing, retain the immediate behavioral repair and investigate the previous request: who was asked, what options and costs were shown, and whether the answer was refusal or inaction. Establish who can alter scope before advising the user to stop commitments. If appropriate, surface the possibility that anger toward a powerful board is landing on less powerful colleagues; show how to notice and interrupt that displacement without diagnosing it. Build a response to the actual authority boundary and the previous failed attempt, rather than telling them to ask again more forcefully.

When a departure decision is already made, keep the fairness/authorization check and clear final communication. Then make the remaining agency real: the departing person may have input on timing within actual constraints, announcement language, recognition, and whether a handoff is appropriate. They cannot veto an authorized decision, and fair terms cannot depend on friendship, public endorsement or reassuring the manager. Don't require an extended handoff to ease the manager's discomfort. Prepare a concise truthful explanation of the decision, give room for reasonable questions without staging a fake appeal, and process the manager's guilt and lessons with someone else afterwards.
